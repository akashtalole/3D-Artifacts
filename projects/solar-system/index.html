    Solar System Explorer¶

    A real-time 3D model of the Solar System, modelled on NASA's Eyes on the Solar System. Every planet sits where it really is today, at true scale, and you can fly from the whole system down to a single world, then speed time up or run it backwards.

    Launch the explorer Source on GitHub

    What you can do¶
    Fly to any world Planet bar, search box (arrow keys + Enter), or click a label or sphere. The camera flies on a logarithmic path, so a trip from the Sun to Pluto takes a couple of seconds.
    Info card Radius, mass, sidereal day, orbital period, axial tilt, plus live distance from the Sun and Earth, light time and orbital speed. Show whole orbit frames the body's orbit; Close-up returns to it.
    Time controls −1 yr/s … real time … +1 yr/s, pause, and LIVE to jump back to now. Keyboard: Space pauses. Dates are limited to 1800–2050, the range of the orbital elements.
    Lighting Flood light lights every world evenly, like Eyes' lighting=flood. Turn it off for natural lighting: the Sun is the only light, so you see day, night and phases.
    Orbits & labels Toggle orbit lines (and the asteroid belt) and labels.
    Deep links #/<body>?lighting=flood\|natural&date=2030-01-01&rate=86400, e.g. #/saturn or #/earth?lighting=natural.

    Earth, natural lighting¶

    The globe is turned by Greenwich sidereal time and tilted to the real axis, so the day–night line matches the actual time.

    Saturn¶

    Rings from 74,500 to 140,220 km with a radial colour and transparency map, tilted 26.7° with the planet. Titan orbits in the ring plane.

    Jupiter¶

    Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto on their real distances and periods.

    How positions are computed¶

    • Planets and Pluto: JPL's Approximate Positions of the Planets (E. M. Standish), Table 1 Keplerian elements and their century rates, J2000 ecliptic. Kepler's equation is solved with Newton iteration. Accurate to arc-minutes for the inner planets, a fraction of a degree for the outer ones, between 1800 and 2050.
    • Moon: a low-precision geocentric series from the Astronomical Almanac (≈ 0.3°). It is tidally locked, so the same face always points at Earth.
    • Other moons: the Galilean moons and Titan move on circular orbits with their real radius and period, in the parent's equatorial plane. Their phase is illustrative, not ephemeris-accurate.
    • Orbital speed: the vis-viva equation, v² = GM☉ (2/r − 1/a).
    • Asteroid belt: 4,000 illustrative particles between 2.2 and 3.3 AU, each moving at its Kepler rate on the GPU.

    Rendering at Solar-System scale¶

    Everything is in kilometres at true size, from a 1,188 km Pluto to Neptune at 4.5 billion km. Two tricks keep it stable:

    1. Floating origin. Positions are kept in double precision in JavaScript. Each frame, the focused body is moved to the scene origin and everything else is placed relative to it, so the GPU never sees huge numbers near the camera.
    2. Logarithmic depth buffer. The camera's near plane is 1 km and its far plane is 2 × 10¹¹ km without z-fighting. Custom shaders (the asteroid belt) include three.js's logdepthbuf chunks.

    Planets are too small to see from far away, so every body also has a fixed-size ring marker and an HTML label, as in Eyes.
